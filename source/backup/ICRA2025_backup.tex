\begin{abstract}
% Challenging but safety-critical scenarios, like hazardous debris or scattered construction sites, demand that robots respond to dynamic environments not only versatilely, but also safely and promptly. Although legged robots excel on rough terrains with proven versatility, a formal guarantee of their safety and reactivity remains a significant challenge, hindering their deployment in urgent applications.

% In this work, we adopt linear temporal logic (LTL)-based reactive synthesis to abstract the terrain-adaptive dynamic locomotion problem as a symbolic level gait design and decision-making problem.
This study proposes a hierarchically integrated planning framework for quadrupedal locomotion over dynamically changing, unforeseen terrains.
Existing footstep planning approaches either rely heavily on heuristics, compromising safety and reliability, or directly solve a holistic optimization, suffering from expensive computation as terrain features become complex.
% \qm{maybe we could fill this up with heuristic methods and their disadvantages}, 
To address this challenge, our approach integrates reactive synthesis and Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP) to leverage the strength of both approaches. Specifically, we employ reactive synthesis to provide formal safety guarantees at the abstraction level and MICP to ensure footstep planning with physical feasibility for each symbolic transition.
% \qm{We should add a few sentences to summarize our approach: a global planner to produce a nominal trajectory. MICP,  symbolic repair to guide the search of gait configuration, and online execution that can generalize to unseen terrain combinations.}
% while leveraging convex optimization to provide global feasibility certificate at the motion planning level. 
% The mismatch between the robot's symbolic-level decisions and its physical-level capabilities in navigating adversarial environments is addressed in both the offline and online phases.
% To address the mismatch between the robot's symbolic-level decisions and its physical-level capabilities in maneuvering through adversarial environments, we propose an offline-online framework. 
% A Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP) generates motion primitves and offers an approximate dynamics-feasibility guarantee based on terrain information given a prior. 
To handle unrealizable specifications due to limited feasible symbolic transitions, we leverage a 
% closed-loop 
symbolic repair process to identify dynamically feasible missing skills in both offline and online phases. %, thereby ensuring that the synthesis remains realizable.
During the online execution phase, we re-run the MICP using real-world terrain data to bridge the gap between offline synthesis and online execution, along with online symbolic repair to handle unforeseen terrains.
Extensive simulations demonstrate the efficacy of our framework in discovering missing locomotion skills and enabling prompt reactions to safety-critical environments, such as scattered stepping stones and rebars.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% New outline:

% 1. The primary motivation for using reactive synthesis in terrain-adaptive locomotion lies in its contributions to safety and computational efficiency. First, reactive synthesis provides a formal method to ensure safety by systematically considering possible interactions between the robot and surrounding adversarial terrains. Second, it abstracts complex and rich features of the environment, enabling faster computation compared to traditional methods that directly reason about the intricate relationship between the environment and robot dynamics.

% 2. Symbolic repair is crucial for guiding the search for necessary gait configurations traversing adversial terrains. One reason is that it reduces the effort needed to identify all essential skills required for different terrains. Additionally, symbolic repair facilitates the accumulation of experience. As repairs are conducted, the robot identifies and stores necessary skills, progressively enhancing its capabilities as it encounters a wider variety of terrains.

% 3. During the online phase, we address the gap between discrete abstraction and real-world information for both the robot and its environment. This is achieved by solving the MICP in real-time using up-to-date robot and terrain data. Online repair also utilizes real-time terrain information to handle previously unseen environments that were not encountered during offline synthesis. Unlike learning-based approaches, which may struggle with out-of-distribution terrains, our method remains adaptable to new conditions.

Terrain-adaptive locomotion is crucial for enhancing the traversing capabilities of legged robots and advancing beyond blind walking \cite{di2018dynamic,kim2019highly, zhou2022momentum}. Existing approaches often use heuristics \cite{fankhauser2018robust,jenelten2020perceptive,agrawal2022vision,grandia2023perceptive, asselmeier2024hierarchical} to simplify foot placement selection given a nominal reference trajectory. However, formal guarantees on locomotion safety, considering the robot's physical capabilities, are rarely explored—despite their importance in safety-critical scenarios like hazardous debris and construction sites. Moreover, complex features of surrounding terrains significantly increase the computational burden for optimization-based methods, such as mixed-integer program (MIP) \cite{valenzuela2016mixed,shirai2022simultaneous,jiang2023locomotion}, which directly address the interplay between terrain segments and robot kinematics and dynamics. Other MIP-based methods rely on simplifications to enhance speed, \textit{e.g.}, assuming predetermined contact sequences and timing \cite{deits2014footstep,ponton2016convex,ding2020kinodynamic,tonneau2020sl1m,risbourg2022real,acosta2023bipedal} or fixing the number of footsteps \cite{aceituno2017simultaneous}, but still face challenges in scaling up with the number of terrain segments.

Motivated by this fact, our study aims to combine formal control methods, \textit{i.e.}, linear temporal logic (LTL)-based reactive synthesis \cite{bloem2012synthesis}, with MIP-based approaches, to safely and promptly react to dynamically changing environments. Continuous robot states and local environments are abstracted into symbolic states to systematically study high-level interactions between the robot and surrounding challenging terrains such as obstacles and large gaps exceeding the robot's physical limit. Then, a Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP) is solved to provide a physical feasibility certificate for each symbolic transition. This integrated framework not only bridges the gap between high-level abstraction and low-level physical capabilities but also alleviates the computational burden of MICP through symbolic-level guidance, focusing on a nominal desired direction and specific regions to explore.
% focusing on a nominal desired moving direction and limiting the number of regions to explore. 
The proposed approach is flexible in that it can be used to follow commands from a global/higher-level planner \cite{wermelinger2016navigation, fernbach2017kinodynamic,norby2020fast,chignoli2022rapid,liao2023walking,feng2023gpf} or from a user, and can incorporate additional locomotion behavior as needed.

Enabling complex reasoning about physical interaction with a terrain-feature-rich environment requires an accurate abstraction of the robot states or terrain. However, this induces a scalability challenge, particularly when evaluating numerous symbolic transitions for underactuated, hybrid systems such as legged robots. Rather than solving for abundant feasible transitions \cite{bhatia2010sampling,liu2013synthesis,maly2013iterative,gu2022reactive} for each terrain combination, it is more computationally efficient to utilize existing motion primitives such as a fixed set of gait configurations, and ensure traversability among a limited set of terrains selected by prior knowledge or experience. In addition, merely deploying the existing proven feasible motion primitives provides another layer of safety.
% it is more computationally efficient to utilize existing motion primitives such as a fixed set of gait configurations, tailored to a limited set of terrains informed by prior knowledge or experience. In addition, deploying the existing proven feasible motion primitives provides another layer of safety. 
However, missing enough transition capabilities
% it 
often leads to \textit{unrealizable specifications}. Therefore, it is essential to develop a method that automatically identifies physically feasible symbolic transitions necessary for legged robots to maneuver in challenging environments. In this work, we adopt a symbolic repair approach based on \cite{pacheck2022physically}, incorporating dynamic feasibility checks, to design missing yet necessary motion primitives for our terrain-adaptive locomotion problem.

During online execution of a synthesized high-level transition strategy, two critical issues may arise, potentially leading to execution failures. First, discrepancies between offline-checked terrain and real-world one are often inevitable, and can lead to a mismatch between the desired symbolic transition and what is physically possible. Second, newly encountered terrains may also make the symbolic specifications unrealizable, as they were not accounted for during offline synthesis. %Existing efforts in the LTL synthesis literature address unexpected environmental changes and robot action failures mainly at the specification level \cite{livingston2012backtracking,ehlers2014resilience,guo2013revising,li2021reactive,zhou2022reactive,cao2022leveraging,meng2024automated}. Studies that consider both robot and environmental mismatches at symbolic and physical levels remain largely underexplored, yet become increasingly crucial for safety-critical, terrain-adaptive locomotion. 

In this work, we solve an MICP online while executing the synthesized transition strategy, using real-world terrain data to ensure motion feasibility and fidelity. More importantly, an online symbolic repair is performed, similar to the offline repair, to identify possible transitions based on the new terrain and updated feasibility provided by the online MICP, allowing the robot to explore previously unseen terrains.
% for progressive generalization to a wider range of terrains.

The primary contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a hierarchically integrated planning framework for terrain-adaptive locomotion by combining reactive synthesis with MICP. This framework generates motion primitives, a computationally expensive process, based on the actual needs of the robot, by leveraging symbolic search over an abstraction to limit the number of required motion primitives.
    %\item To address unrealizable specifications due to limited predefined motion primitives, we introduce a symbolic repair approach that incorporates dynamic feasibility checks, and automatically identifies the missing but necessary transitions for legged robots to effectively navigate adversarial terrains.
    \item During online execution, we address the disparity between offline synthesis and real-world terrain conditions at both physical and symbolic levels. Our approach leverages an online MICP solver along with an online symbolic repair process to account for real-world terrain discrepancies and newly encountered conditions, enhancing motion feasibility and safety during execution.
    % \item The entire framework is implemented in an online manner and validated through both simulation and hardware experiments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-time local planner that determines both gait and foothold with a safety guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Specifications}
We use the Generalized Reactivity(1) (GR(1)) fragment of Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)~\cite{bloem2012synthesis}
% has been widely used 
to encode task specifications.
% ~\cite{bloem2012synthesis}.
% and automatically synthesize the system's transition behaviors. 
GR(1) specifications $\spec$ 
% is constructed from atomic propositions $\pi \in AP$, which is evaluated to be ${\rm True}$ or ${\rm False}$ and 
follow the grammar 
$\spec \: := \var \:|\: \neg\: \spec \:|\: \spec \:\land\: \spec \:|\: \bigcirc\spec \:|\: \square \spec \:|\: \lozenge \spec$,
where $\var \in \ap$ is an atomic proposition,
$\neg$ ``not'' and $\land$ ``and'' are Boolean operators,
and
$\bigcirc$ ``next'',
$\square$ ``always'',
and $\lozenge$ ``eventually''
are temporal operators.
% . Boolean operators $\neg$ ``not'' and $\wedge$ ``and'' in addition to a set of temporal operators $\ocircle$ ``next'', $\mathcal{U}$ ``until'', and $\mathcal{R}$ ``release'', are denoted. To be concise, we omit the derivations of other boolean operators such as $\vee$ ``or'', $\rightarrow$ ``implies'', and $\leftrightarrow$ ``if and only if'', as well as temporal operators $\Diamond\,\varphi$ ``eventually $\varphi$'' and $\square\,\varphi$ ``always $\varphi$''. 
We refer the readers to~\cite{baier2008principles} for a detailed description of LTL.
% A detailed description of LTL can be referred to \cite{baier2008principles}.

GR(1) specifications are expressed in the form of 
$\spec = \envspec \to \sysspec$,
where $\envspec = \envinit \land \envsafety \land \envlive$ 
is the assumptions on the behaviors of the possibly adversarial environment,
and $\sysspec = \sysinit \land \syssafety \land \syslive$
is the guarantee of the robot's behaviors.
% Specifically,
For $\alpha \in \{e, s\}$,
$\alphainit, \alphasafety, \alphalive$
characterize the 
initial conditions,
safety constraints,
and liveness requirements
respectively.
%
We divide the environment safety assumptions $\envsafety$ into skill assumptions $\envsafetynothard$,
which describe the postconditions of the skills (see Sec.~\ref{sec:spec_gen}),
and hard assumptions $\envsafetyhard$ that cannot be modified
($\envsafety = \envsafetynothard \land \envsafetyhard$).
Similarly, we split the safety guarantees $\syssafety$ into skill guarantees $\syssafetynothard$,
which represent the preconditions of the skills,
and hard guarantees $\syssafetyhard$ that cannot be modified
($\syssafety = \syssafetynothard \land \syssafetyhard$).

\subsection{Symbolic Repair}\label{sec:symbolic_repair}
The specification $\spec$ generated in Sec.~\ref{sec:spec_gen} may be unrealizable
if the physical feasibility checking in Sec.~\ref{sec:motion_primitive} does not generate enough skills for the robot to reach all goal locations over all possible terrain combinations.
%
To adress this issue, we leverage the ideas in~\cite{pacheck2022physically} 
% to ensure that the generated specification $\spec$
% (Sec.~\ref{sec:spec_gen}) is realizable~\cite{pacheck2022physically}.
% The specification $\spec$ can be unrealizable if the physical feasibility checking (Sec.~\ref{sec:motion_primitive}) does not generate enough skills for the robot to traverse to all goal locations over all possible terrain combinations.
% The repair 
to
generate
new skill suggestions necessary for the robot to accomplish the task.
%over all possible terrain environments.
Different from~\cite{pacheck2022physically}, we assume that skills do not contain intermediate states,
and the skill preconditions
are over both the controllable centroidal inputs $\inpcen$ and uncontrollable terrain inputs $\inpterrain$, 
while the postconditions remain to contain 
controllable
centroidal inputs $\inpcen$ only.

% We extend repair to handle skills whose preconditions are over both the controllable centroidal inputs $\inpcen$ and terrain inputs $\inpterrain$ while the postconditions still only contain controllable inputs $\inpcen$.

For any subset $\statevar\subseteq \ap$, we say that $\statevar$ is a winning state
if the robot can guarantee to satisfy the task from $\statevar$
despite adversarial environment behaviors.
The repair process systematically expands the set of winning states until
it intersects with all liveness goals and contains all initial states.
To this end, the repair alternatively modifies the preconditions and postconditions of existing skills.
When modifying preconditions, the repair randomly selects a skill whose postcondition is winning, and modifies its precondition to a non-winning state, thus making the new precondition possible to win.
When modifying postconditions, the repair randomly selects a skill whose preconditions and postconditions are both non-winning, and modifies its postcondition to a winning state, thus making the original precondition possible to win.
%
Fig~\ref{fig:preliminary_nine_squares}c illustrates the use of repair in Example~\ref{example:1}. 
To create a skill that reaches the requested grid $(x_0, y_0)$, the
repair selects the skill transition that moves the robot from the grid $(x_1, y_0)$ to $(x_2, y_0)$,
and modifies the postcondition to reach $(x_0, y_0)$ instead.

We attempt to implement the suggested skills by reconfiguring the gait in terms of contact sequence to generate new motion primitives. This can be achieved either by leveraging human knowledge to introduce new candidate gait parameters or by solving a gait-free version of Eq.~\ref{ocp:mip_general}. The latter involves introducing additional binary variables to determine contact states, which, however, requires a longer computation time.
If any suggested skill is not feasible, 
we add the symbolic transition as a hard constraint on repair,
and repeat the repair process.

\subsection{Offline Synthesis and Repair Case Study}
Similar to Example~\ref{example:1}, we define additional terrain combinations for the purpose of evaluating offline synthesis and repair process. 
Fig.~\ref{fig:offline_terrains} shows four 9-grid terrain combinations with varying stepping stone sparsities. The first row presents the real-world terrain polygons, while the second row displays the corresponding symbolic-level maps. The grid size is chosen based on factors such as robot size and terrain dimensions. In all examples, we use a 0.6 m cell size, but this can be adjusted for practical applications. 
We use a 1.5-second trotting gait as the motion primitive given ahead of time, and the MICP planner has a 0.02s timestep, resulting in $N=75$ knot points, and $R=8$ terrain polygons per transition. In Fig.~\ref{fig:offline_repair}, the pink arrows indicate the original feasible transitions. The robot successfully navigates upward and downward across all terrains; terrain case (a) achieves all transitions and is not shown in the figure due to space limit. 
The infeasible transition arises when moving sideways on sparse stepping stones, restricted by the leg kinematics constraint in Eq.~\ref{eq:rom}. However, specifications for terrain case (b) and (c) remain realizable as the robot avoids sideways movement by taking detour steps, indicating that not all skills are required for these types of  terrains. Such a coincidence does not apply to terrain case (d), where the specification becomes unrealizable due to physically unreachable $(x_1,y_0)$ and $(x_1,y_2)$. This unrealizability triggers a symbolic repair to identify a symbolic suggestion in \todo seconds, adding two additional transitions (blue arrows). By reconfiguring to a bounding gait with short aerial phases, the robot restores transition feasibility, making terrain case (d)'s specification realizable.

\section{Conclusion}
In this work, we presented an integrated planning framework for terrain-adaptive locomotion that combines reactive synthesis with MICP, enabling safe and reactive responses in dynamically changing environments. 
% To address unrealizable specifications arising from limited motion primitives, we introduced a symbolic repair approach that incorporates dynamic feasibility checks, automatically identifying missing transitions necessary for navigating adversarial terrains. In the online execution phase, we tackled the disparity between offline synthesis and real-world conditions by using an online MICP solver and an online symbolic repair process based on real-world terrain data. New terrain conditions encountered during execution. 
Our approach not only enhances motion feasibility and safety but also provides a scalable solution for legged robots maneuvering in complex and safety-critical environments. Future work will involve extensive hardware testing to validate the proposed framework in real-world scenarios. Additionally, we plan to explore specifications of greater complexity to further test the algorithmic scalability.
