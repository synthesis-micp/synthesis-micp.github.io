\begin{abstract}
% Challenging but safety-critical scenarios, like hazardous debris or scattered construction sites, demand that robots respond to dynamic environments not only versatilely, but also safely and promptly. Although legged robots excel on rough terrains with proven versatility, a formal guarantee of their safety and reactivity remains a significant challenge, hindering their deployment in urgent applications.

Existing terrain-adaptive locomotion approaches either rely heavily on heuristics for reactive foothold selection, compromising safety and reliability, or directly solve a holistic optimization, suffering from expensive computation as terrain features become complex.

Moreover, complex features of surrounding terrains significantly increase the computational burden for optimization-based methods, such as mixed-integer program (MIP) \cite{valenzuela2016mixed,shirai2022simultaneous,jiang2023locomotion}, which directly address the interplay between terrain segments and robot kinematics and dynamics. Other MIP-based methods rely on simplifications to enhance speed, \textit{e.g.}, assuming predetermined contact sequences and timing \cite{deits2014footstep,ponton2016convex,ding2020kinodynamic,tonneau2020sl1m,risbourg2022real,acosta2023bipedal} or fixing the number of footsteps \cite{aceituno2017simultaneous}, but still face challenges in scaling up with the number of terrain segments.

\textbf{Paragraph 3: We propose an integrated framework that combines synthesis + MICP to solve (locomotion with formal guarantee), faster than MIP solution. Two-stage framework.}

Motivated by this fact, our study aims to combine formal control methods, \textit{i.e.}, linear temporal logic (LTL)-based reactive synthesis \cite{bloem2012synthesis}, with MIP-based approaches, to safely and promptly react to dynamically changing environments. Continuous robot states and local environments are abstracted into symbolic states to systematically study high-level interactions between the robot and surrounding challenging terrains such as obstacles and large gaps exceeding the robot's physical limit. Then, a Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP) is solved to provide a physical feasibility certificate for each symbolic transition. This integrated framework not only bridges the gap between high-level abstraction and low-level physical capabilities but also alleviates the computational burden of MICP through symbolic-level guidance, focusing on a nominal desired direction and specific regions to explore.

Enabling complex reasoning about physical interaction with a terrain-feature-rich environment requires an accurate abstraction of the robot states or terrain. However, this induces a scalability challenge, particularly when evaluating numerous symbolic transitions for underactuated, hybrid systems such as legged robots. Rather than solving for abundant feasible transitions \cite{bhatia2010sampling,liu2013synthesis,maly2013iterative,gu2022reactive} for each terrain combination that potentially involve complex contact selection, it is more computationally efficient to utilize existing contact plans such as a fixed set of locomotion gait configurations, and ensure traversability among a limited set of terrains selected by prior knowledge or experience. In addition, merely deploying the existing proven feasible locomotion gaits provides another layer of safety.

However, missing enough transition capabilities

often leads to \textit{unrealizable specifications}. Therefore, it is essential to develop a method that automatically identifies physically feasible symbolic transitions necessary for legged robots to maneuver in challenging environments. In this work, we adopt a symbolic repair approach based on \cite{pacheck2022physically}, incorporating dynamic feasibility checks, to design missing yet necessary locomotion gaits for our terrain-adaptive locomotion problem.

During online execution of a synthesized high-level transition strategy, two critical issues may arise, potentially leading to execution failures. First, discrepancies between offline-checked terrain and real-world one are often inevitable, and can lead to a mismatch between the desired symbolic transition and what is physically possible. Second, newly encountered terrains may also make the symbolic specifications unrealizable, as they were not accounted for during offline synthesis. %Existing efforts in the LTL synthesis literature address unexpected environmental changes and robot action failures mainly at the specification level \cite{livingston2012backtracking,ehlers2014resilience,guo2013revising,li2021reactive,zhou2022reactive,cao2022leveraging,meng2024automated}. Studies that consider both robot and environmental mismatches at symbolic and physical levels remain largely underexplored, yet become increasingly crucial for safety-critical, terrain-adaptive locomotion. 
In this work, we solve an MICP online while executing the synthesized transition strategy, using real-world terrain data to ensure motion feasibility and fidelity.

% is constructed from atomic propositions $\pi \in AP$, which is evaluated to be ${\rm True}$ or ${\rm False}$ and

$\spec \: := \var \:|\: \neg\: \spec \:|\: \spec \:\land\: \spec \:|\: \bigcirc\spec \:|\: \square \spec \:|\: \lozenge \spec$,
where $\var \in \ap$ is an atomic proposition,
$\neg$ ``not'' and $\land$ ``and'' are Boolean operators,
and
$\bigcirc$ ``next'',
$\square$ ``always'',

GR(1) specifications are expressed in the form of 
$\spec = \envspec \to \sysspec$,
where $\envspec = \envinit \land \envsafety \land \envlive$ 
is the assumptions on the behaviors of the possibly adversarial environment,
and $\sysspec = \sysinit \land \syssafety \land \syslive$
is the guarantee of the robot's behaviors.

$\alphainit, \alphasafety, \alphalive$

initial conditions,
safety constraints,

respectively.

and hard assumptions $\envsafetyhard$ that cannot be modified

Similarly, we split the safety guarantees $\syssafety$ into skill guarantees $\syssafetynothard$,
which represent the preconditions of the skills,
and hard guarantees $\syssafetyhard$ that cannot be modified
($\syssafety = \syssafetynothard \land \syssafetyhard$).

The offline synthesis module 
% $\inpstateterrainposs \subseteq 2^\inpterrain$,
% and 
generates a strategy

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_offline_online}(a),
this module takes in
a set of request goals and
a set of possible terrain states from prior knowledge of the workspace. In addition, a set of locomotion gaits is also provided by the user. After discretizing the local environment and characterizing the terrains in Sec.~\ref{sec:preliminaries_abstraction}, we first solve a gait-fixed MICP problem to determine the feasibility of each potential skill given the locomotion gaits
(Sec.~\ref{sec:motion_primitive}), and then encode all feasible transitions as skills in a specification

If the specification is realizable, we generate a strategy for online execution.

Fig.~\ref{fig:micp}
shows our MICP formulation, where the decision variables $\vg \phi$ include base position $\v r$, velocity $\dot{\v r}$, acceleration $\ddot{\v r}$, orientation $\vg \theta$ parameterized by Euler angle and its derivative $\dot{\vg \theta}$, individual 
end-effector
(EE)
position $\v p_j$, velocity $\dot{\v p}_j$, and acceleration $\ddot{\v p}_j$, and individual contact force $\v f_j$ for the foot $j$. The cost function consists of tracking costs and regularization terms governed by diagonal matrices $\v Q$ and $\v R$. 
The tracking costs include the deviation from a desired base trajectory interpolating a path between the terrain grid locations defined in each skill.
For any natural number $n \in \naturals$,
we denote the set $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ as $\range{n}$.
We use $N$, $n_f$, and $n_s$, and $R$ to represent the number of timesteps, the number of feet, the number of footsteps specified by the gait configuration for each foot, and the number of convex terrain polygons to be considered, respectively. For example,  Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_offline_online}(b) shows a case with four polygons. Note that, during the offline phase, the terrain polygons are predefined and homogeneous, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:preliminary_nine_squares}(a).

% In Eq.~\ref{eq:dynamics},
We encode the system dynamics as a single rigid body in Eq.~\ref{eq:dynamics},
using a simplified Euler equation to keep the dynamics constraint convex. To incorporate safe region constraints to select proper footholds, we introduce binary variables $\v H_{r,k,j}$, with $r$, $k$, and $j$ expressing the $r^{\rm th}$ convex region, $k^{\rm th}$ footstep, and $j^{\rm th}$ foot. 
Eq.~\ref{eq:safe_region} restricts
the robot's EE to stay within one of the convex polygons. 
Frictional constraints are defined in Eqs.~\ref{eq:force_non_zero} - \ref{eq:force_friction_cone}, with $\v n$ as the normal vector of the terrain corresponding to the position $\v p^{xy}_j$ and $\mathcal{C}_j$ as the set of timesteps indicating stance for the $j^{\rm th}$ foot. Contact constraints in Eqs.~\ref{eq:ee_velocity_zero} - \ref{eq:force_zero} check the given contact states and activate at different timesteps.
Since the joint angles are omitted in the single rigid body model, we use a fixed Jacobian at a nominal joint pose to approximately consider the torque limit constraint in Eq.~\ref{eq:max_torque}. 
Lastly, the kinematics constraint in Eq.~\ref{eq:rom} strictly limits the possible EE movements to assure safety.

For any subset $\statevar\subseteq \ap$, we say that $\statevar$ is a winning state
if the robot can guarantee to satisfy the task from $\statevar$
despite adversarial environment behaviors.
The repair process systematically expands the set of winning states until
it intersects with all liveness goals and contains all initial states.
To this end, the repair alternatively modifies the preconditions and postconditions of existing skills.
When modifying preconditions, the repair randomly selects a skill whose postcondition is winning, and modifies its precondition to a non-winning state, thus making the new precondition possible to win.
When modifying postconditions, the repair randomly selects a skill whose preconditions and postconditions are both non-winning, and modifies its postcondition to a winning state, thus making the original precondition possible to win.
%

To create a skill that reaches the requested grid $(x_0, y_0)$, the
repair selects the skill transition that moves the robot from the grid $(x_1, y_0)$ to $(x_2, y_0)$,
and modifies the postcondition to reach $(x_0, y_0)$ instead.

We implement the suggested skills by reconfiguring the gait in terms of contact sequence to generate new motion primitives. This can be achieved either by leveraging human knowledge to introduce new candidate gait parameters or by solving a gait-free version of Eq.~\ref{ocp:mip_general}. The gait-free MICP retains all continuous decision variables and constraints from the gait-fixed MICP but modifies the contact state-dependent constraints, as the contact sequence and timing are no longer fixed. Instead of using $\v H_{r,k,j}$, we introduce a different set of binary variables $\v H_{r,m,j}$ for defining the contact state for each foot at each time step explicitly similar to \cite{valenzuela2016mixed}. $r$ and $j$ still represent the $r^{\rm th}$ convex region and $j^{\rm th}$ foot, respectively, while $m \in \range{M}$ denotes the $m^{\rm th}$ time step, evenly discretized with $\Delta t_m$ over the entire $M$ time steps. Therefore, the gait-free MICP involves introducing additional binary variables to determine contact states, which, however, requires a longer computation time.
If any suggested skill is not feasible, 
we add the symbolic transition as a hard constraint on repair,
and repeat the repair process.

\subsubsection{Rebar}
Inspired by the labor-intensive rebar tying task on construction sites, we explore a second application where the quadrupedal robot navigates a rebar mat. Each rebar is modeled as a rectangular polygon with a 3 cm width. Unlike the stepping stone scenario, this setup offers more potential stepping regions due to the higher rebar density. Observing that the robot's traversability depends on rebar sparsity in different directions—whether aligned with its facing direction or not given a tolerance—we abstract and classify rebar types based on a combination of horizontal sparsity (perpendicular to the robot's facing direction) and vertical sparsity (parallel to it). Each direction's sparsity is categorized as \textit{none}, \textit{single}, \textit{dense}, \textit{sparse}, or \textit{extreme sparse}, determined heuristically by the number and relative spacing of the rebars inside the abstraction cell. To reduce problem complexity, some trivial combinations are removed such as \textit{none} or \textit{single} in both directions. As a result, we define two example specifications with 7 and 14 rebar terrain types and corresponding real scenarios. The 7-type scenario generates random rebar sparsity between 0.15 and 0.35 m, excluding the extreme sparse type (above 0.4 m). In contrast, the 14-type scenario includes rebar sparsity from 0.15 to 0.6 m, potentially covering more non-trivial combinations including the extreme sparse ones.
