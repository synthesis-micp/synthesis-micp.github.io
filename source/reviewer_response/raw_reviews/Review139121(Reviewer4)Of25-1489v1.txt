Before going into the details of the paper, I will first raise what
appears to be a problem with respect to the new T-RO rules:

"your manuscript must not be just a mere extension of the previous
version(s) filling in, e.g., details of proofs or additional
corollaries, additional experiments, or more detailed background and
narrative. Rather, the new submission must contain new results of
substantive research significance and impact beyond the previous
papers."

The work is clearly an extension of [24] and re-uses some of its
figures and analysis.

The claimed online retargeting mechanism appears to be already present
in prior MICP-based online execution approaches, including [24], and is
mainly reframed rather than newly introduced here. The main new results
seem to be the (very nice) experiments on hardware, but the associate
editor should decide whether in itself these already constitute a
significant contribution or not.

The remaining claimed new contribution with respect to [24] is the
study of the addition of a rotation (yaw angle), but it is not
demonstrated in the companion paper, only in Figure 10, which suggests
the work is only preliminary (no quantitative analysis is reported
either).

Anyways, here is the review of the paper.

The paper proposes to reduce the combinatorial complexity of Mixed
Integer approaches for locomotion planning by adding a layer of
abstractions in the planning to limit the planning horizon while
considering long-term objectives. A symbolic planner works at the high
level and is coupled with different forms of MICP problems, more or
less challenging, to verify the validity of the plan.
The approach is interesting, and the results qualitatively strong.
However, the novelty of the paper (wrt to [24]) focuses on hardware
experiments and reactivity, but there are insufficient details on this
aspect to get a clear idea of what is implemented by the authors. The
quantitative analysis does not really allow comparing methods with each
other either, and would benefit from rewriting.

+ While the authors claim that the solutions are dynamically
consistent, it is essential to be clear about the fact that angular
momentum is completely neglected by the approach. This is a well-known
limitation of MIP solvers, making their use tricky for any form of
truly dynamic motions, or for applications to humanoid robots. This is
not clearly stated, and the claims of feasibility should be toned down.
In particular, when the yaw is included as a variable in the user
study, this limitation should become apparent and it is not discussed.

+ "the online gait-fixed MICP is executed given accurate terrain
information from a terrain segmentation module". We definitely need
more information on that module!

Section VII.A.2 raises several concerns. The section introduces
collision-free regions for obstacle avoidance, but the paper does not
specify how obstacles are detected or how these regions are constructed
from sensor or terrain data. It is also unclear which parts of the
robot are considered for collision checking. Figure 5 does not provide
sufficient clarification. The description suggests that collision-free
regions are selected rather than explicit collision avoidance being
enforced, yet the section then discusses swing-foot height
parametrization, which implies stepping over obstacles—something that
is not demonstrated in the results. Overall, this section is confusing
and would benefit from substantial clarification. Fig 14 is more
informative, but it does not seem trivial to identify clear collision
regions with off-the-shelf perception tools. More importantly, it is
not clear how the obstacles affect the definition of the feasible
convex regions in the plan. The discussion suggests manual labelling is
done when classifying terrain, but is it also the case that the
collision map is manually updated or not?  In any case, it is not
entirely clear in the current form how this replanning was not already
enabled by the previous framework.

More importantly, although this is not a perception paper, the
presentation implicitly suggests that identifying convex collision-free
regions online is straightforward. In general, when terrain surfaces do
not exhibit such regular structure, this is not the case. For instance,
[108] does not reliably provide convex regions -contrary to what the
name of the module implies-. The authors should either clarify the
perception tools and assumptions used to obtain these regions or tone
down the emphasis on online perception capabilities. In the current
stage, the reviewer has no idea how the convex polygons are actually
accurately built, obstacles detected, and appropriate regions sorted.

+ The orientation change is not demonstrated in the companion video,
only in Figure 10. Including orientation is definitely a significant
challenge and interesting avenue of research, which brings a variety of
questions, principally around the combinatorial and the limitations of
the abstract representation of the map in terms of turn and repairs
necessary. The authors claim in the title of the section that they
demonstrate generalizability, but the work is very preliminary and
nothing is really demonstrated. I would suggest removing this section
entirely, or being clearer on the results obtained.

+ Results:
How is the MIP implemented? With which solver?
Table III and Fig 9 are the only quantitative results regarding the
comparisons of the MIP and the proposed framework, but they do not
compare the same scenarios nor the same thing. While it is, I guess,
obvious that the proposed approach would be faster, it would clarify
things to compare the global running time of the proposed on each
scenario, with a proper decomposition of the time spent in each module.
Obtaining the actual computation times upon reading the paper proves
challenging because the information is disseminated. There are no
quantitative data on the amount of repair necessary, etc.

What if a short-horizon MPC was used to repeatedly solve the proposed
scenarios in a receding-horizon fashion, with a fixed gait for the
rebar scenario? It might be interesting to see whether the problem is
solved and whether computation time is favourable to the authors.
Without such comparisons, the interest of using a long-term planner is
not demonstrated. I assume that the scenarios proposed contain local
minima that would make a nominal planner fail, but this is not clear
from the current results.
